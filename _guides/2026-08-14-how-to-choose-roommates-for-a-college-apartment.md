**How to Choose Roommates for a College Apartment** 🚨
====================================================================

Finding the right roommate can feel like solving a puzzle where the pieces are sleep schedules, snack preferences, and that mysterious person who always leaves the dishes in the sink. I’ve been there—juggling late‑night study sessions, part‑time jobs, and the hope that my future housemate won’t turn the living room into a drum circle at 2 a.m. Getting this right matters because a good roommate makes rent feel lighter, chores easier, and college life a lot more fun. Let’s walk through a practical, friendly process to help you pick someone who’ll be a true partner in crime (the good kind).

## Quick Context
This guide is for college students (or anyone sharing a lease) who are hunting for an apartment through **Decent Apartments** and want to avoid the dreaded “roommate nightmare.” Whether you’re signing a lease for the first time or renewing for another year, the steps below work for any budget, city, or school schedule. Remember: fair‑housing laws protect you from discrimination based on race, religion, gender, family status, disability, and more—so keep your criteria focused on lifestyle and habits, not protected characteristics.

## Start with Your Non‑Negotiables
Before you even post a roommate ad, get clear on what you absolutely can’t live without. Think about sleep habits, cleanliness standards, noise tolerance, and guest policies. Write them down; they’ll become your screening filter.

> **💡 Pro Tip:** Rank your top three must‑haves (e.g., “quiet after 10 p.m.,” “no pets,” “splits grocery bill evenly”) and ask potential roommates to rate how well they match each one on a scale of 1‑5. It turns a vague feeling into concrete data.

## Screen for Compatibility (Habits, Study Style, Social Life)
Compatibility isn’t just about liking the same music; it’s about how you both function day‑to‑day. Ask about class schedules, study routines, and how they unwind. A night‑owl who parties until 3 a.m. might clash with an early‑rising lab student, even if they’re both fun people.

> **⚠️ Watch Out:** Don’t assume “friendly” means “compatible.” Someone who loves hosting big gatherings might still be tidy and respectful—just make sure their social rhythm aligns with yours (or that you’re both okay with occasional compromises).

## Talk Money Upfront
Money conversations can feel awkward, but they save a lot of headaches later. Discuss rent split, utility estimates, internet costs, and how you’ll handle shared purchases (like cleaning supplies or toilet paper). If one person expects to cover more because they have a larger room, get that agreement in writing (even a simple email thread works).

> **🎯 Key Insight:** Use a shared spreadsheet or app (like Splitwise) to track expenses in real time. Transparency builds trust and prevents the “who owes what” surprise at month‑end.

## Test the Vibe with a Casual Hangout
A quick coffee or a short walk lets you see chemistry beyond text messages. Observe how they communicate, whether they listen, and if you feel comfortable being yourself. If you can’t meet in person, a video chat works—just keep it light and focused on getting to know each other’s rhythms.

> **💡 Pro Tip:** Bring up a hypothetical scenario (e.g., “What would you do if you found a leak in the bathroom at midnight?”) to gauge problem‑solving style and sense of responsibility.

## Positive Signs and Negative Signs
Knowing what to look for helps you spot a good fit early—and avoid red flags.

**Positive Signs**
- They ask thoughtful questions about your schedule and preferences.
- They mention specific habits (like “I wipe down the kitchen after cooking”) that show self‑awareness.
- Their communication is prompt, clear, and respectful.
- They’re excited about sharing chores but also open to discussing a fair split.

**Negative Signs**
- They dodge direct questions about cleanliness, noise, or guests.
- They seem overly eager to move in without discussing logistics or finances.
- Their stories about past roommates always end in blame (“They were impossible”) with no self‑reflection.
- They pressure you to decide quickly, citing “another interested party” without giving you time to think.

## What to Check Before You Decide
Before you sign a lease together, run through this quick checklist:

- **Lease Details:** Confirm that all roommates are listed on the lease (or that you have a legally sound roommate agreement if only one person signs).  
- **Utilities:** Verify which utilities are included in rent and estimate the rest (electricity, water, internet).  
- **Guest Policy:** Clarify how long guests can stay and whether overnight partners need approval.  
- **Conflict Resolution:** Agree on a simple process for addressing issues (e.g., a weekly check‑in or a shared note board).  
- **Exit Plan:** Discuss what happens if someone needs to move out early—notice period, finding a replacement, and any financial responsibilities.  
- **Safety:** Ensure the building has working locks, smoke detectors, and that you both feel safe walking to and from campus at night.

## Key Takeaways
- **Know your must‑haves** and use them as a screening filter.  
- **Talk money early**—use apps to keep expenses transparent.  
- **Assess compatibility** through habits, schedules, and a casual hangout.  
- **Watch for red flags** like vague answers, blame‑shifting, or pressure to rush.  
- **Document agreements** (lease, utilities, chores) to prevent misunderstandings later.  
- **Keep fair‑housing principles** in mind—focus on lifestyle, not protected characteristics.
